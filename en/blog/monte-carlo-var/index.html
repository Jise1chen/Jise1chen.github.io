RISK MANAGEMENT

Estimating VaR with Monte Carlo simulation

Ziqing (Stephen) ChenOct 9, 2026

Introduction

The question risk managers hear most often is: under normal market conditions, how much can this portfolio lose? Value at Risk (VaR) answers it with a single number, which is why it became the common language of risk for banks, funds and regulators.

The hard part of computing VaR is estimating the tail of the profit-and-loss distribution. When a portfolio holds only linear positions and returns are roughly normal, one formula is enough. Add options or path-dependent products, or switch to a more realistic price model, and the closed form disappears. Monte Carlo simulation takes the direct route: if the distribution cannot be written down, simulate it and read the quantile off the sample.

This note defines VaR, derives the simulation steps under geometric Brownian motion, implements the single-asset and multi-asset cases in Python, and closes with simulation error and the limits of the method.

What VaR is

VaR is a quantile of the loss distribution: for a given holding period and confidence level α, the probability that the loss exceeds VaR is at most 1 − α. Writing the loss over the holding period as L = V0 − VT,

VaRα(L)=inf⁡{ ℓ∈ℝ:P(L>ℓ)≤1−α }\mathrm{VaR}_\alpha(L) = \inf\{\, \ell \in \mathbb{R} : P(L > \ell) \le 1 - \alpha \,\}

For example, “the 10-day 99% VaR is CNY 86,000” means that over the next 10 trading days the chance of losing more than CNY 86,000 is only 1%. It says nothing about how large the loss is in that 1% of cases, a point we return to below.

Three methods are in common use. They differ in how they obtain the P&L distribution.

MethodWhere the distribution comes fromStrengthWeakness
Parametric (variance–covariance)Assume normal returns and apply a formulaFastest; closed formBreaks down for non-linear positions and fat tails
Historical simulationUse past returns directly as future scenariosNo distributional assumptionLimited to the history window; what never happened never appears
Monte Carlo simulationSpecify a stochastic model and generate many scenariosModels and products can be arbitrarily complexComputationally heavy; results depend on the model

The steps of Monte Carlo VaR

The method breaks into five steps. Changing the model only touches the first two.

  1. Choose a price model and estimate its parameters (drift, volatility, correlations).
  2. Use random numbers to generate N price paths, or N terminal prices directly.
  3. Revalue the portfolio in each scenario to get its terminal value VT.
  4. Compute the loss in each scenario, L = V0 − VT.
  5. Take the α-quantile of the N losses. That is the VaR estimate.

The standard starting model is geometric Brownian motion (GBM), which assumes the asset price follows

dSt=μSt dt+σSt dWtdS_t = \mu S_t\,dt + \sigma S_t\,dW_t

where μ is the annualized drift, σ the annualized volatility and W a standard Brownian motion. Applying Itô’s lemma to ln S gives the exact solution for the terminal price:

ST=S0exp⁡ ⁣[(μ−12σ2)T+σT Z],Z∼N(0,1)S_T = S_0 \exp\!\left[\left(\mu - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right)T + \sigma\sqrt{T}\,Z\right], \qquad Z \sim N(0,1)

Two things are worth noticing. First, the exponent contains μ − σ2/2 rather than μ. The extra term is the Itô correction, and dropping it is the most common beginner mistake. Second, because GBM has an exact solution, a single standard normal draw reaches the horizon in one step, with no need to slice the holding period. Step-by-step discretization is only needed when the model has no exact solution or the product is path-dependent.

Python implementation: a single asset

Start with the simplest case: a CNY 1,000,000 position in one stock with 8% annualized drift and 20% annualized volatility, and a 10-trading-day VaR at the 99% confidence level.

import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm

def mc_var_single(V0, mu, sigma, T, alpha, n_sims, seed=None):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    z = rng.standard_normal(n_sims)
    # Exact GBM solution: one step to the horizon
    VT = V0 * np.exp((mu - 0.5 * sigma**2) * T + sigma * np.sqrt(T) * z)
    loss = V0 - VT                      # losses are positive
    var = np.quantile(loss, alpha)      # alpha-quantile of the loss distribution
    es = loss[loss >= var].mean()       # average loss beyond VaR
    return var, es

V0, mu, sigma, T, alpha = 1_000_000, 0.08, 0.20, 10 / 252, 0.99
var, es = mc_var_single(V0, mu, sigma, T, alpha, 100_000, seed=42)

With 100,000 simulations the VaR comes out at CNY 86,970, and the expected shortfall (ES) computed alongside it at CNY 99,329.

The advantage of this example is that a closed form exists to check against. The loss is a monotone function of Z, so substituting the 1% quantile of Z into the exact solution is enough:

VaRα=V0[1−exp⁡ ⁣((μ−12σ2)T+σT Φ−1(1−α))]\mathrm{VaR}_\alpha = V_0\left[1 - \exp\!\left(\left(\mu - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right)T + \sigma\sqrt{T}\,\Phi^{-1}(1-\alpha)\right)\right]
exact = V0 * (1 - np.exp((mu - 0.5 * sigma**2) * T
                         + sigma * np.sqrt(T) * norm.ppf(1 - alpha)))

The closed-form value is CNY 86,346, about 0.7% away from the simulated one. Whenever you write a Monte Carlo program, check it this way against a special case with a known answer before moving on to problems that have none.

A multi-asset portfolio: handling correlation

With several assets, the key is to make the simulated random numbers preserve the correlations between them. The standard approach is a Cholesky factorization of the correlation matrix, ρ = LL⊤, with L then used to turn independent standard normals into correlated ones:

𝐙=L 𝜺,𝜺∼N(𝟎,I)  ⟹  Cov(𝐙)=LL⊤=ρ\mathbf{Z} = L\,\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}, \qquad \boldsymbol{\varepsilon} \sim N(\mathbf{0}, I) \;\Longrightarrow\; \mathrm{Cov}(\mathbf{Z}) = LL^{\top} = \rho

The example below is a CNY 1,000,000 three-asset portfolio with weights of 50%, 30% and 20%.

def mc_var_portfolio(V0, w, mu, sigma, corr, T, alpha, n_sims, seed=None):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    L = np.linalg.cholesky(corr)
    # one scenario per row; right-multiplying by L.T applies L @ eps to each
    z = rng.standard_normal((n_sims, len(w))) @ L.T
    growth = np.exp((mu - 0.5 * sigma**2) * T + sigma * np.sqrt(T) * z)
    VT = V0 * (growth @ w)              # aggregate terminal asset values at initial weights
    loss = V0 - VT
    var = np.quantile(loss, alpha)
    es = loss[loss >= var].mean()
    return var, es

w     = np.array([0.5, 0.3, 0.2])
mu    = np.array([0.08, 0.06, 0.10])
sigma = np.array([0.20, 0.15, 0.30])
corr  = np.array([[1.0, 0.6, 0.3],
                  [0.6, 1.0, 0.2],
                  [0.3, 0.2, 1.0]])

var, es = mc_var_portfolio(1_000_000, w, mu, sigma, corr, 10 / 252, 0.99, 100_000, seed=42)

The portfolio’s 10-day 99% VaR is CNY 69,630 and its ES is CNY 78,946.

Computing VaR for each asset on its own and adding them up gives CNY 88,355. The portfolio VaR is CNY 18,725 lower, about 21%, and that gap is the risk reduction from diversification. As a cross-check, the parametric (delta-normal) method gives CNY 71,204, about 2% from the simulated value. The difference comes from the linear approximation the parametric method applies to a lognormal distribution.

One practical caveat: Cholesky factorization requires a positive-definite matrix. A correlation matrix estimated from history can fail that test when there are many assets and a short sample, in which case it has to be repaired first (for example by finding the nearest positive-definite matrix).

Simulation error and convergence

A Monte Carlo VaR is itself a random variable. Change the seed and the result changes, with an error that shrinks at rate 1/√N. The asymptotic standard error of a sample quantile is

SE ⁣(VaR^α)≈1f(qα)α(1−α)N\mathrm{SE}\!\left(\widehat{\mathrm{VaR}}_\alpha\right) \approx \frac{1}{f(q_\alpha)}\sqrt{\frac{\alpha(1-\alpha)}{N}}

where f(qα) is the loss density at the quantile. The higher the confidence level, the smaller the tail density and the larger the error: a 99.9% VaR needs far more simulations than a 99% one.

As an experiment on the single-asset example, each N was run with 200 different seeds, recording the mean and standard deviation of the VaR estimates (closed-form value: CNY 86,346).

Each tenfold increase in N cuts the standard deviation to roughly a third, in line with the theoretical 1/√N rate. At N = 1,000 the 99% quantile is determined by the 10 largest samples alone, and a 5% standard deviation makes the result close to unusable.

Two recommendations for practice. First, report a standard error or confidence interval alongside the VaR, estimated by repeating over several seeds or by bootstrap. Second, when the compute budget is tight, antithetic variates, importance sampling and quasi-Monte Carlo (Sobol sequences) all reduce the error substantially at the same N. Importance sampling is especially effective for tail quantiles.

Limitations and improvements

The quality of a Monte Carlo VaR is capped by the model: more simulations only compute a possibly wrong distribution more precisely. Three issues deserve the most attention.

Fat tails

GBM assumes normally distributed log returns, while real returns have visibly fatter tails. Replacing the normal shock with a Student-t with 4 degrees of freedom, rescaled to unit variance so the volatility stays the same, is a one-line change:

nu = 4
z = rng.standard_t(nu, n_sims) * np.sqrt((nu - 2) / nu)   # rescale to unit variance

With every other parameter as in the single-asset example, 100,000 simulations give the following.

Confidence levelNormal-shock VaR (CNY)t(4)-shock VaR (CNY)Difference
99%86,34697,395+13%
99.9%113,733174,480+53%

The two models have exactly the same volatility, yet the gap widens the further into the tail you go. The normal assumption understates risk precisely where accuracy matters most.

Volatility is not constant

GBM treats σ as a constant, whereas real volatility clusters and is negatively correlated with price. There are two families of improvement: GARCH-type models that update the conditional volatility day by day, and stochastic volatility models such as Heston that let the variance follow its own random process. Neither generally has an exact solution for the terminal price, so the holding period has to be simulated in small steps, and this is exactly where Monte Carlo has the edge over the parametric method.

Flaws in VaR as a measure

VaR only says where the quantile is. It does not describe how large losses are beyond it. It is also not subadditive: under some distributions the VaR of a portfolio can exceed the sum of the VaRs of its parts, contradicting the intuition that diversification reduces risk.

Expected shortfall (ES) addresses both points. It is the conditional mean of the loss given that it exceeds VaR:

ESα(L)=E[ L∣L≥VaRα(L) ]\mathrm{ES}_\alpha(L) = E\left[\,L \mid L \ge \mathrm{VaR}_\alpha(L)\,\right]

The code above already computes ES along the way, and in a Monte Carlo framework it costs almost nothing extra. The Basel Committee’s revised market risk framework (FRTB) has likewise replaced 99% VaR with 97.5% ES as the capital measure under the internal models approach.

Summary

Monte Carlo VaR is a five-step framework: choose a model, generate scenarios, revalue, compute losses, take the quantile. Its value is flexibility, since the model and the products can be swapped as needed while the framework stays the same.

Three things to keep in mind when using it:

  • Check the code against a special case with a closed form before tackling harder problems.
  • Simulation error falls at rate 1/√N, so report a standard error with the result.
  • Model risk outweighs simulation error: the distributional assumption affects tail results far more than the number of simulations.
